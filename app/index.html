ICE Detention Architecture Under Load

How does a building that holds people respond as stays get longer and more people are held inside?

A holding room built for an afternoon is a different place after three days. A housing unit built for sixty is a different place with a hundred and ten.

This is an interactive model of immigration detention interiors. Choose a kind of facility, say how well its architecture supports daily life, then push on it with longer stays and more people. Watch where support holds, where it thins, and where it gives way.

What this is

An imaginative instrument built from abstract types of facility. It is not a map, a rating, or a judgment of any real facility, and it makes no predictions. It can show architecture holding up as well as failing.

Four ways to look

Grid
Thirteen architectural capacities against ten parts of daily life. Each cell asks a question, and you answer it with a baseline from 0 to 4.
Network
The same scenario as a web, showing which parts of life depend on a single capacity.
Person
One faceless figure in a half-cut room, through a representative day, thinking short first-person thoughts.
Equation
The math for one cell, worked out in full, with every number explained.

The idea

Support is not a fixed property of a building. It is a margin between what the building supplies and what the people inside need, and that margin moves. Demand rises with time and crowding and levels off below a ceiling. Where it passes a cell's support, the cell turns to strain. The building has not changed, only the demand placed on it.

Where the numbers come from

None of them were measured. They were reached by interpreting reporting and inspection records (interviews, site visits, oversight findings, agency standards) and written down precisely so they can be argued with. If you know a building type better than we do, change the numbers.
