# Architecture Under Load: Compact Research Packet

## Purpose

This packet is the compact source backbone for the **Architecture Under Load** project: a parametric model of immigration-detention interiors that asks how architectural form interacts with **duration of exposure** and **population/capacity load**.

It is not a bibliography of every facility discussed in the research. Instead, these sources were selected because together they supply six different kinds of evidence the model needs:

1. the structure and heterogeneity of the national detention system;
2. ICE's own intended detention standards;
3. comparative observations across multiple facilities using a common state oversight framework;
4. a recent case of rapid population growth stressing conventional detention facilities;
5. a recent soft-sided mass-detention architecture in which spatial design itself becomes unusually visible; and
6. cross-facility evidence about segregation, sensory conditions, duration, and isolation.

The model should be read against the friction among these source types rather than treating any one source as a complete description of ICE detention.

---

## 1. U.S. Government Accountability Office — system and inspection architecture

**U.S. Government Accountability Office. _Immigration Detention: DHS Should Define Goals and Measures to Assess Facility Inspection Programs._ GAO-25-107580. May 21, 2025.**

**Primary URL:**  
https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107580

**Full web report:**  
https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107580/index.html

### Why it matters

This is the best compact source for understanding the detention network as a **heterogeneous system rather than a single architectural type**. GAO examined DHS inspection activity for facilities holding people longer than 72 hours during fiscal years 2022–2024 and describes facilities operated by ICE, private contractors, and state/local entities.

For the parametric model, it supports several foundational decisions:

- facility type and operating arrangement must remain variables rather than assumptions;
- a passing inspection does not imply absence of significant deficiencies;
- environmental health/safety, medical care, food service, sanitation, communication, segregation, and other systems can fail independently;
- different oversight bodies inspect different slices of the environment using different methods.

GAO reported that ICE's Office of Detention Oversight rated facilities acceptable or better in 238 of 241 inspections during FY2022–24 while still identifying deficiencies. This is one reason the model should **not** reduce an environment to a single pass/fail or humane/inhumane score.

### Method / evidentiary character

Federal performance audit using DHS/ICE documents and inspection data, interviews with agency officials and selected facility operators, and analysis of inspection programs.

### Caution

GAO's purpose is to evaluate the inspection system, not to reconstruct the sensory experience of confinement. It is strongest for **system structure, oversight, standards, and recurring deficiency categories**, and weaker for phenomenology.

## 2. ICE Performance-Based National Detention Standards — intended operating environment

**U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. _Performance-Based National Detention Standards 2011_, revised December 2016 (PBNDS 2011).**

**PDF:**  
https://www.ice.gov/doclib/detention-standards/2011/pbnds2011r2016.pdf

### Why it matters

PBNDS provides the model's principal **normative baseline**: what ICE itself says a detention environment should provide. Relevant sections include:

- Environmental Health and Safety;
- Food Service;
- Medical Care;
- Personal Hygiene;
- Special Management Units;
- Staff-Detainee Communication;
- Use of Force and Restraints;
- Visitation;
- Grievance System;
- Law Libraries and Legal Materials;
- Transfers.

The standards are useful for distinguishing three things that should never be collapsed in the model:

1. **architectural capacity** — what the form can physically support;
2. **operational provision** — what staff actually make available;
3. **observed lived condition** — what detained people experience.

A facility may contain showers yet provide inadequate shower access. It may contain an outdoor recreation area yet severely restrict access to it. It may have a medical unit whose effective distance is increased by queues, escorts, searches, or staffing shortages.

### Method / evidentiary character

Agency standards rather than observational research.

### Caution

PBNDS describes intended requirements, not proof of actual conditions. It should be used as a **design/operations reference point**, never as evidence that a particular facility complies with those requirements.

## 3. California Department of Justice — comparative facility family

### 2025 report

**California Department of Justice. _Immigration Detention in California 2025_ — focus on mental health.**

**Publication portal:**  
https://oag.ca.gov/publications/

**Immigration detention portal:**  
https://www.oag.ca.gov/immigrant

### 2026 report

**California Department of Justice. _Immigration Detention in California 2026: A Review of Conditions of Confinement._**

**Publication portal:**  
https://oag.ca.gov/publications/

**Immigration detention portal:**  
https://www.oag.ca.gov/immigrant

**2026 release / summary:**  
https://oag.ca.gov/node/623373

### Why this source family matters

These reports are particularly valuable because they compare **multiple facilities under a relatively consistent observational framework** rather than concentrating on one notorious site.

The 2025 report reviews six California facilities:

- Adelanto ICE Processing Center;
- Desert View Annex;
- Mesa Verde ICE Processing Facility;
- Golden State Annex;
- Imperial Regional Detention Facility;
- Otay Mesa Detention Center.

The 2026 report adds **California City Detention Facility**, a reactivated correctional facility, and examines the system after substantial population growth during 2025.

For the model, this source family is especially useful for:

- comparing architectural and operational variation across sites;
- seeing how the same broad detention standards produce different lived environments;
- studying **population stress** across an existing network;
- medical capacity and mental-health access;
- recreation and movement;
- sanitation and hygiene;
- temperature and maintenance;
- communication and due process;
- the consequences of rapidly reopening or expanding facilities.

The 2026 report is particularly important because California DOJ explicitly connects worsening conditions with rapid population growth and strained resources. That makes it unusually relevant to the model's **Population / Capacity Load** control.

### Method / evidentiary character

State government oversight involving site visits, document review, interviews, and evaluation against ICE detention standards.

### Caution

The California sample is geographically concentrated and cannot stand in for the national network. Its strength is **comparability across several facilities**, not national representativeness.

## 4. Human Rights Watch + Americans for Immigrant Justice + Sanctuary of the South — Florida under acute load

**Human Rights Watch, Americans for Immigrant Justice, and Sanctuary of the South. _“You Feel Like Your Life Is Over”: Abusive Practices at Three Florida Immigration Detention Centers Since January 2025._ July 21, 2025.**

**Full report:**  
https://www.hrw.org/report/2025/07/21/you-feel-like-your-life-is-over/abusive-practices-at-three-florida-immigration

### Facilities examined

- Krome North Service Processing Center;
- Broward Transitional Center;
- Federal Detention Center in Miami.

### Why it matters

This is the key source for thinking about **load as a multiplier** rather than merely another condition.

The report documents a rapid rise in detention population and describes conditions including:

- severe overcrowding;
- people sleeping on concrete floors without normal bedding;
- very cold holding spaces;
- continuous fluorescent lighting;
- inadequate hygiene and shower access;
- medical-care delays and medication problems;
- food problems;
- transfers and communication disruption;
- restrictions on legal and psychosocial support.

For Architecture Under Load, the crucial insight is systemic:

> Population growth can simultaneously increase pressure on horizontal sleeping space, toilets, showers, food service, medical throughput, recreation, staff attention, communication, and movement.

Those are therefore not independent sliders. The model's population variable should expose **latent architectural coupling**.

This source also helps distinguish **processing architecture** from ordinary housing architecture. A space designed for temporary intake can become a radically different environment when duration stretches from hours into days.

### Method / evidentiary character

Interviews with currently and recently detained people, family members, immigration lawyers, facility visits to Krome and Broward, documentary review, and population-data analysis. The report states that researchers documented the experiences of 17 immigrants across the three facilities and also interviewed 14 attorneys.

### Caution

This is human-rights investigative reporting focused on alleged and documented failures during an acute expansion period. It is intentionally oriented toward identifying abuses, not constructing a representative national average. Use it for **failure modes and load interactions**, not as a default description of every ICE facility.

## 5. Human Rights Watch — Fort Bliss / Camp East Montana and soft-sided mass architecture

**Human Rights Watch. _“You're Only Getting Out Deported or Dead”: Abusive US Immigration Detention at Ft. Bliss._ July 15, 2026.**

**Full report:**  
https://www.hrw.org/report/2026/07/15/youre-only-getting-out-deported-or-dead/abusive-us-immigration-detention-at-ft

**Spanish edition:**  
https://www.hrw.org/es/report/2026/07/15/solo-saldras-deportado-o-muerto/detencion-abusiva-de-inmigrantes-en-ft-bliss

### Why it matters

Camp East Montana makes architectural relationships unusually legible because it is a **rapidly deployed soft-sided mass-detention environment**, not simply another conventional jail or prison.

HRW describes a facility with capacity for up to 5,000 people, consisting of five soft-sided tent-like structures divided into pods. Reported conditions include large communal groups, sanitation problems, medical neglect, barriers to legal representation, and other abuses.

For the architectural model, this report was especially productive because it foregrounds questions such as:

- How large is the smallest inhabitable social unit?
- Can sound move freely between nominally separate spaces?
- Are sleeping, eating, sanitation, and social activity adequately separated?
- Can moisture, odor, dust, temperature, or noise propagate through shared environmental systems?
- Does the architecture provide daylight and exterior orientation?
- How resilient is a rapidly deployed structure to long-duration habitation?

This source drove several model variables that were less obvious in conventional prison architecture:

- **spatial granularity**;
- **sensory compartmentalization**;
- **acoustic refuge / acoustic topology**;
- **wet-core design**;
- **functional adjacency**;
- **daylight and exterior orientation**;
- **program-to-building fit**.

### Method / evidentiary character

Human Rights Watch interviews with detained people plus review of documents, court records, government records, policies, and other materials. HRW says all interviewees gave informed consent and that it sought responses from DHS, DOD, and contractors before publication.

### Caution

As with the Florida report, this is an investigation of serious alleged/documented abuses rather than a neutral architectural survey. Its special value to this project is that the **physical form itself is unusually explicit**, allowing operational testimony to be connected to spatial organization.

## 6. Physicians for Human Rights + Harvard — segregation, duration, and sensory deprivation

**Physicians for Human Rights; Harvard Law School Immigration and Refugee Clinical Program; Peeler Immigration Lab / Harvard Medical School. _“Endless Nightmare”: Torture and Inhuman Treatment in Solitary Confinement in U.S. Immigration Detention._ February 6, 2024.**

**Full report:**  
https://phr.org/our-work/resources/endless-nightmare-solitary-confinement-in-us-immigration-detention/

### Why it matters

This is the packet's strongest source for understanding the interaction between **architecture, sensory conditions, isolation, and duration**.

Unlike a single-facility investigation, the report combines national ICE records with interviews spanning many facilities. Its FOIA-derived dataset included records from 125 facilities, while interview participants had experienced detention across dozens of facilities in multiple states.

For the model it is especially useful for:

- segregation / solitary-confinement architecture;
- prolonged exposure;
- lighting and circadian disruption;
- limited human contact;
- recreation and movement restriction;
- communication restriction;
- medical and mental-health access;
- the distinction between ordinary detention and a radically altered sensory regime inside the same institution.

The report found more than 14,000 solitary-confinement placements in ICE records from 2018–2023, with an average duration of approximately 27 days. This makes it valuable for thinking about **duration not simply as elapsed time but as a force that changes the significance of environmental conditions**.

A fluorescent light, small room, or lack of social contact has one meaning for an hour and another after days or weeks.

### Method / evidentiary character

Two complementary methods:

1. analysis of ICE and other federal records obtained through FOIA and litigation; and
2. structured qualitative interviews with 26 formerly detained people who had experienced solitary confinement.

The underlying quantitative work therefore provides a broader institutional frame around the interview testimony.

### Caution

The report is specifically about solitary confinement and should not be generalized to ordinary housing units. Its value is precisely that it shows how **changing the confinement regime inside a facility changes nearly every sensory and social parameter without necessarily changing the larger building**.

# How the six sources work together

These sources should not be flattened into one evidence pool. They answer different questions.

| Source | Primary question it answers | Model contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **GAO 2025** | What is the detention/inspection system structurally? | Heterogeneity, oversight, recurring deficiency categories, rejection of one aggregate score |
| **ICE PBNDS** | What does ICE say facilities should provide? | Normative baseline; separates intended provision from observed condition |
| **California DOJ 2025/26** | How do multiple facilities differ under a common review framework? | Comparative architecture/operations; population-growth stress |
| **HRW Florida 2025** | What happens when conventional facilities experience acute population pressure? | Load multiplier; processing-vs-housing mismatch; cascading failures |
| **HRW Fort Bliss 2026** | What becomes visible in rapidly deployed soft-sided mass detention? | Spatial granularity, sensory coupling, wet-core, acoustic and adjacency variables |
| **PHR/Harvard 2024** | What happens when confinement becomes prolonged and spatially/socially isolating? | Duration, sensory deprivation, segregation, circadian/social effects |

Together they create a useful triangulation:

### Standards versus conditions

ICE PBNDS tells us what the institution says should exist. Government inspections and state oversight show where actual facilities comply or fail. Interviews and human-rights investigations show what those differences can feel like from inside.

### Cross-section versus stress event

GAO and California DOJ provide broader cross-sections. Florida and Fort Bliss provide high-stress cases in which system relationships become easier to see.

### Architecture versus operation

Fort Bliss makes physical form unusually visible. PBNDS makes intended operation visible. California and Florida show how architecture and operations interact under changing load.

### Population versus duration

Florida is especially informative about **population pressure**. PHR/Harvard is especially informative about **duration of exposure**. Their combination provides the conceptual basis for the two global stress controls in Architecture Under Load.

# Recommended reading order for a coding/modeling agent

A coding agent does not need to read every source cover-to-cover before prototyping. Use this order:

1. **Read this packet first.** Understand what each source is for.
2. **GAO 2025:** understand the heterogeneous system and why a single facility score is inappropriate.
3. **ICE PBNDS:** skim the table of contents and relevant environmental/care sections to understand the intended baseline.
4. **HRW Florida:** study population pressure and cascading failures.
5. **HRW Fort Bliss:** study architectural coupling and soft-sided mass form.
6. **PHR/Harvard:** study duration, segregation, sensory exposure, and isolation.
7. **California DOJ 2025/26:** use as the comparative check against overfitting the model to spectacular failure cases.

The coding agent should not translate individual allegations directly into coefficients. The reports establish **relationships and plausible sensitivities**; the numerical coefficients in the prototype remain explicit design hypotheses until separately validated.

# Research rules for the model

When translating this packet into Architecture Under Load:

- Do not treat an advocacy report, government inspection, agency standard, or detainee interview as interchangeable evidence.
- Preserve uncertainty.
- Do not infer that a condition reported at one facility is universal.
- Do not infer actual compliance from the existence of an ICE standard.
- Do not infer absence of problems from a facility's overall passing inspection rating.
- Treat population and duration as **stressors that interact with architecture**, not as automatic proxies for abuse.
- Keep architectural capacity separate from operational access.
- Keep unobserved/unknown separate from poor.
- Do not create a single moral, legal, or humane/inhumane score.
- Allow the model to represent resilient architecture as well as failure.

The objective is not to reproduce a particular detention center. It is to turn documented relationships among **form, load, duration, access, and lived conditions** into an inspectable parametric model.
